1 · Adjectives vs Adverbs

What does the word describe?

An adjective describes a noun, pronoun or state. An adverb usually describes a verb — how an action happens.

ADJECTIVE → noun / state
ADVERB → action

Adjective

She is careful.
It looks difficult.

Common after be, seem, look, feel, sound, become.

Adverb

She works carefully.
He answered quickly.

It tells you how the action happens.

Same idea, different job

Many adjective/adverb pairs come from the same word family. The form changes because the function changes.

quiet → quietly
The room is quiet.
They worked quietly.
patient → patiently
The teacher is patient.
She explained it patiently.
successful → successfully
The project was successful.
They completed it successfully.

Remember that an adjective can also come before a noun: a difficult question, an efficient system. An adverb usually cannot replace it there.

Exam trap: After linking verbs, you often need an adjective: She looks tired, not tiredly.
Find the word being described. Noun/state → adjective. Action → adverb.

2 · Adjectives with Prefixes

Prefixes often create an opposite or negative meaning.

There is no single universal negative prefix, so learn common adjective families and let meaning guide you.

un-happy → unhappy
usual → unusual
in-correct → incorrect
complete → incomplete
im-possible → impossible
patient → impatient
ir- / il-regular → irregular
legal → illegal
dis-honest → dishonest
organized → disorganized
non-essential → nonessential
mis-leading → misleading
over-confident → overconfident

Build word families, not isolated words

Prefixes are especially useful in multiple-choice questions because one option may have the right base adjective but the wrong prefix. Learning common families helps you eliminate impossible forms quickly.

regular → irregular
The schedule is irregular.
patient → impatient
He became impatient.
honest → dishonest
The report was dishonest.

Some prefixes can also add a different idea rather than simple negation: overconfident means too confident; misleading means giving the wrong impression.

Do not invent forms such as unpossible, inhonest or discorrect.

3 · Adverbs of Frequency

Frequency tells you how often something happens.

These adverbs often appear with the Simple Present because they describe habits and routines.

always
≈100%
usually
≈80–90%
often
≈60–70%
sometimes
≈40–50%
hardly ever
≈5–10%
never
0%

With most verbs

subject + frequency adverb + main verb
I usually review my answers.

With be

subject + be + frequency adverb
She is often tired.

Frequency expressions

once a week, twice a month, every day, from time to time often come at the end.

I review vocabulary twice a week.

Position can change with sentence structure

With ordinary verbs, put the frequency adverb before the main verb. With be, put it after be. In questions, the adverb normally appears after the subject.

Ordinary verb
They often travel for work.
Verb be
He is rarely late.
Question
Do you usually study at night?

Sometimes can also appear at the beginning or end for emphasis: Sometimes I study at night.

4 · Adverbs of Manner

They answer “How?”

Many use adjective + -ly, but spelling changes and irregular forms matter.

AdjectiveAdverbExample
carefulcarefullyShe checked carefully.
easyeasilyHe completed it easily.
automaticautomaticallyThe system updates automatically.
goodwellShe speaks English well.
fastfastHe works fast.
hardhardThey study hard.

Placement of manner adverbs

A manner adverb often comes after the verb or after the object of the verb. Avoid separating a verb from its object unnecessarily.

After the verb
She smiled politely.
After the object
He answered the question confidently.
With emphasis
She carefully checked the form.

Some words have the same adjective and adverb form, such as fast, hard, early and late.

hard ≠ hardly. “He works hard” = with effort. “He hardly works” = he almost does not work.

5 · Comparatives

Use comparatives to compare two.

The form depends mainly on adjective length and spelling.

TypePatternExample
Short adjective-er + thansmaller than
Ends in -yy → i + -ereasier than
CVC spellingdouble consonant + -erbigger than
Long adjectivemore + adjective + thanmore difficult than
Irregularspecial formbetter · worse

Comparatives can also show degree

Words such as much, a lot, far, a little can modify a comparative and show how big the difference is.

This option is much cheaper.
The new system is a little faster.
The second text is far more difficult.

You can also express equality with as + adjective + as: This test is as difficult as the previous one. For inequality: not as ... as.

Avoid double comparatives: more easier ✗ / more better ✗.
The word than is a strong comparative clue.

6 · Superlatives

Use superlatives for one item at the highest or lowest degree in a group.

Comparative = two. Superlative = one item compared with the whole group.

TypePatternExample
Short adjectivethe + -estthe fastest
Ends in -yy → i + -estthe easiest
Long adjectivethe most + adjectivethe most interesting
Irregularspecial formthe best · the worst

Notice the group or range

A superlative normally identifies one item inside a larger group. The group may be explicit or understood from context.

She is the youngest person on the team.
This is the most useful section in the guide.
It was the worst answer of the three.

A very common exam pattern is one of the + superlative + plural noun: one of the most important topics.

Group clues: of the three, in the class, I have ever seen.

7 · Quantifiers

Start with noun type.

First decide whether the noun is countable plural or noncount. Then decide whether the meaning is large quantity, some quantity or almost none.

QuantifierCount pluralNoncountMeaning
many✓—large number
much—✓large amount
a few✓—some, positive
few✓—almost none
a little—✓some, positive
little—✓almost none
a lot of✓✓large quantity
some / any✓✓indefinite quantity

many vs much

many questions
much time

a few vs few

a few friends = some
few friends = almost none

a little vs little

a little time = some
little time = almost none

some vs any

some questions
any questions?

Meaning matters as much as noun type

A few and a little mean “some” and usually sound positive or sufficient. Few and little emphasize that the quantity is very small and often insufficient.

a few
We have a few options, so we can choose.
few
We have few options, so the situation is difficult.
a little / little
There is a little time left. / There is little time left.

Much and many are especially common in negatives and questions. In affirmative conversational English, a lot of is often more natural: We have a lot of work.

Exam strategy: noun type → sentence type → meaning.

8 · too / enough / so / such

Learn the pattern, not only the translation.

These forms are often tested together because they appear around adjectives and nouns but use different word order.

StructureUseExample
too + adjectiveexcess / problemtoo heavy
adjective + enoughsufficient degreebig enough
enough + nounsufficient quantityenough time
so + adjective/adverb + thatdegree + resultso difficult that...
such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + thatnoun phrase + resultsuch a difficult test that...
so many / so much + nounhigh quantityso many people

Compare the meaning carefully

Too usually expresses a problem or excess. Enough expresses sufficiency. So and such emphasize degree, often followed by a result clause with that.

too
The room is too small for the group.
enough
The room is large enough for the group.
so / such
It was so useful that I saved it.
It was such a useful guide that I saved it.

Also notice too much + noncount noun and too many + plural count noun: too much traffic, too many questions.

Word order matters: adjective + enough but enough + noun.
Only adjective? Think so. Adjective + noun phrase? Check such.
